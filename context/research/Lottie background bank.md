## Her opinions — where she takes a side

Consolidated from the topic sections below, so a drafter can scan her sharpest stances in one
place rather than hunting across sections. Cross-referenced, not duplicated in full — see the
linked section for context.

- **AI:** "I strongly think it's the marketer's fault" when AI-assisted work goes wrong. AI is a
  tool, not to blame. See [[#AI opinions and material]].
- **AI:** the environmental cost is real, but it's "big tech and government's" problem to solve,
  not an individual's — the personal opportunity cost of opting out on principle is too high.
- **AI:** an informed "no" (genuinely understands it, chooses not to use it) deserves respect; a
  fearful or ignorant "no" doesn't.
- **Data:** "There's a real risk we only see the data that's telling us what we want to hear" —
  fight the data that agrees with you, don't just mine it for a business case. See
  [[#On brand management and training]].
- **Data:** big businesses drown in data nobody uses; small businesses treat it with reverence
  because the investment in it is material.
- **Training:** most marketing courses aren't "fit for purpose for real life," and are "really
  boring." Built Brand Manager Bootcamp because she got properly trained at P&G and most people
  never do.
- **Customer insight:** "However advanced your insights department is, there's no substitute for
  face-to-face" — proximity to real customers beats research budget.
- **Leadership:** the jump to manager is a different job, not a bigger version of the one you
  already have. Most people stall because they keep doing the old job harder.

## How she phrases things (structural moves, distinct from banked facts above)

- Opens with a personal confession or origin story, never a hook gimmick.
- States a hard opinion as a short causal line, not a hedge (e.g. "You're too good at your current
  job. That's why you're not getting the next one.").
- Validates the smart version of an objection and dismisses only the naive one — engages the
  counter-argument rather than strawmanning it.
- Borrows a real, specific anecdote from someone else (a friend, a colleague) when it's more
  concrete than her own experience — never invented or composited into a generic "someone told me"
  story.
- Deflates overthinking or procrastination with a mocking, everyday metaphor (the couch-to-5K
  jogging bit: worrying about gels and trainers before you've even left the house).
- Hedges plainly on genuinely unknowable things ("I don't have a crystal ball" on future token
  costs), but never hedges on an actual opinion. The "no hedging" TOV rule is about opinions, not
  genuinely uncertain facts — don't over-apply it into false confidence about something nobody can
  know yet.
- Closes a strong point by tying it to why she personally cares, not just stating the point and
  stopping (candidate pattern, untested in written copy — see `copy-learnings.md`).

## How she speaks (tone/delivery qualities)

- Dry, self-deprecating, undercuts her own authority ("I'm an AI addict, but I'm not an AI
  expert.").
- Provocative but self-aware about it in speech — names her own view as "radical" or
  "intentionally bullish" before pushing it. **Note the gap between speech and writing here:** in
  written copy this reads as "here's my honest opinion" throat-clearing and should be cut per the
  banned pattern already in `copy-learnings.md`. A spoken habit, not permission to reintroduce it.
- Warm and collective ("I want us to," not "you should").
- Comfortable admitting she doesn't know something outright, rather than fake-confident guessing.

## AI opinions and material ("Why you shouldn't be sleeping on AI" Up Talk, Aug 2026)

- **Self-description: "AI addict, not an AI expert."** "I'm not an AI expert at all. Like, I'm
  really not that good at it yet, but I am totally fascinated and convinced that it's going to
  change... the way all of us work beyond recognition."
- **Her core thesis for this talk:** the industry has "a healthy level of awareness, but an
  unhealthy level of procrastination" about AI. Nearly everyone uses an LLM daily, almost nobody
  has automated anything in their personal life, and the gap between the two is the real problem,
  not a lack of information.
- **"AI debt"**: her term for the compounding cost of not investing in AI now. "I think there's an
  AI debt growing for all of us, both on a personal and organizational level. And as the time goes
  by, that debt gets more and more expensive."
- **Three skills she says will set people apart within a year:** how well you communicate (real
  relationships, building rapport), how well you make decisions (both what you prompt for and
  critically reviewing AI output), and tolerance for fascination with AI ("not how good you are at
  AI... but tolerance for lack of fascination is really quickly waning").
- **On blame when AI-assisted work goes wrong:** "I strongly think it's the marketer's fault... We
  are using tools. AI is a tool. If we start allowing a dialogue that it's in charge, the world is
  quite a scary place." Reviewing AI output critically is, in her view, becoming the actual job.
- **On the environmental objection to AI use:** treats it as real but not an individual's problem
  to solve. "The environmental impact is real. However, it needs to be solved by big tech and by
  government, not by individuals," and the personal opportunity cost of opting out on principle is
  too high.
- **Respects a specific opposing position:** someone who is "really geeky about it," understands
  AI deeply, and chooses not to use it in certain workflows is taking an informed stance she
  respects. The position she pushes back on is opting out from a place of fear or lack of
  understanding, not an informed no.
- **Netflix anecdote** (from a friend who works there, told to Lottie directly): the team is
  spending so much time in LLM training they're running out of time to do their actual work, and
  the quality of written communication is dropping because everything is ChatGPT output — so
  people are phoning each other more, because "the only way through it is to pick up the phone and
  say to someone, what do you think."
- **Tiler/tradesperson metaphor** (built on with a member called Katie in the session, but it's
  become her own line): a tiler's client doesn't care what trowel they use, only the output — the
  same logic applies to using AI as a marketer, you're being paid for the result, not the manual
  labour.
- **"Resource allocators becoming token allocators"** — a line she credits to someone else who
  described it to her, but repeats as her own framing: "we're still managing resource, it's just
  moved from man hours to digital bandwidth."
- **Team Taylor / MCP story:** paid to build a custom Claude connector into their applicant
  tracking system (Team Taylor); the vendor shipped their own native connector about six weeks
  later, making the custom build briefly redundant. She doesn't regret it: "this is all part of the
  cost of staying ahead of the problem."
- **Internal AI examples she's used before:** an AI-built onboarding portal that updates line
  managers on new starters' progress automatically; a member-behaviour dashboard (built by Liv)
  that gives Up Club a single view of member activity across mentoring, talks, huddle-ups and
  Slack, replacing what used to be guesswork.
- Confirms she runs **Up World, Brand Hackers, and Up Talent** (a recruitment business) — three
  businesses, stated in her own words on this call.
- Was seriously considering hiring **a permanent head of AI** to support the transformation, as of
  this talk.
- Uses the printing press and the black-cab-vs-Uber shift as her go-to historical analogies for
  people resisting new technology.

## On brand management and training ("How To Be a Great Brand Manager" Up Talk, panel with two Marketing Accelerator alumni)

- **Pillars she builds live through the panel for what makes a great brand manager:** project
  management, influence, actively staying inspired (with real structures in place, not just "be
  inspired"), understanding the commercial context of the business, knowing your numbers/data, and
  proximity to real customers.
- **"Really excellent brand management is the stuff that has a vision that's going to drive real
  change."**
- **Big business vs. startup data take:** when she left corporate, everyone asked "what are you
  going to do without the data?" Her real experience: "everything I thought would change didn't
  happen and everything I didn't think was going to happen, happened." Her read: big businesses
  have so much data nobody uses it; small businesses treat data with reverence because the
  investment in it is material to the business.
- **On data honesty:** "There's a real risk we only see the data that's telling us what we want to
  hear." The skill is fighting for truth in the data over supporting your own agenda, while still
  being able to use data to build a business case. Both, not either.
- **On customer proximity:** "However advanced your insights department is, there's no substitute
  for face-to-face... getting really intimate with your customers to hear it as raw as possible."
  That's what separates a good brand manager from a great one.
- **Why she built Brand Manager Bootcamp:** "I feel incredibly privileged that I started my career
  at P&G and got trained in this stuff. I actually got taught what brand management is." Built the
  course to offer that to people who never got it.
- **On most marketing courses:** "I don't think they're fit for purpose for real life, and I think
  they're really boring. If you're going to invest in training and development, it should be an
  absolute joy."
- **Confirmed course structure facts:** Brand Manager Bootcamp is for assistant/marketing managers
  who feel untrained; format is live lectures, technical deep dives, group coaching, a weekly
  private podcast interview with a brand manager, senior brand manager or head of marketing, and a
  graduation dinner. The Marketing Accelerator is senior brand management to head-of level, with a
  strategy/leadership focus.
- **Her personal stake, stated directly:** "It's quite shocking how many people have never had any
  investment in their training... I feel really strongly about it, which is a lot of why I do what
  I do."
