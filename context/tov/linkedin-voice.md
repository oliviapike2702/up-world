## 1. Anatomy of the post

Four parts. Treat it as the underlying shape, not a template to fill. `[SOLID]`

1. **Hook** — one or two lines, standing alone with white space after. The only part anyone sees
   before "see more".
2. **Body** — the case. Two or three specific proof points, or the story beats that build to the
   insight. Short sentences, each on its own line.
3. **The turn** — the single line that reframes it.
4. **Statement close** — one clear idea, landed flat.

## 2. The hook

Must do one of three things: surprise the reader, name a problem they're feeling right now, or
promise a specific reward for reading on.

The test: **if your hook could sit at the top of a hundred other posts, it isn't a hook.**

- Open opinionated. A claim, a confession, a contrarian line. Something you can defend.
- **Never open on a rhetorical question**, and never on a warm-up line.
- A strong real quote can lead directly. Don't manufacture a cold-open anecdote just for hook
  value — check whether the quote alone earns the spot first. `[SOLID]` (Liv cut an invented
  HR-director anecdote and led with the quote.)
- Hook categories to reach for when stuck: curiosity, contrarian, story, listicle, transformation.
  Templates in `../../marketing/Socials/LinkedIn Hook Templates.rtf` — starting points, not
  finished lines. Specificity is what makes them work.

## 3. Pain points: be specific or don't bother

Make the reader feel seen by naming the exact problem, in the language they'd use complaining about
it to themselves.

- ❌ "Marketing is hard."
- ✓ "You spend three hours writing a post that gets twelve likes from your friends and one comment
  from a bot account."

Name the specific behaviour, not the abstract problem. "They copy one they saw their manager make"
lands harder than "they copy what they've seen before". `[SOLID]`

## 4. Actionable value

Teach something the reader could apply within 24 hours.

- ❌ "Be authentic."
- ✓ "Open every post with a sentence shorter than ten words."

Ground it in a real named example or a real quote. Never "industry experts say".

## 5. Format — the mechanics that decide whether it lands

- **Length: 1,250 to 3,000 characters.** Under ~1,250 is the most common failure — it reads as a
  diary jotting. A four-line post isn't a draft at 80%, it's unfinished. If short, the fix is
  almost always more body: another proof point, more of the feeling, the bit thrown away in one
  line. `[SOLID]`
- **Line breaks: lots.** Aim for 14+ short paragraphs, most of them one line, generous white space.
  **This applies in the Up World "we" voice too, not just Lottie's** — err toward more breaks than
  feels natural in prose. `[SOLID]` (Liv's Ruth Fittock edit broke almost every sentence onto its
  own line.)
- **Avoid both opposite failures.** Not a column of clipped three-word stubs ("No formal invite. No
  calendar hold. A text."), which kills the story and reads as AI. And not dense prose paragraphs,
  which isn't the voice at all. Every line is a **real sentence** that moves the story forward, with
  air around it.
- **Named frameworks become stacked list lines**, one element per line, never a run-on sentence.
  It signals "this is a framework" and scans. `[SOLID]`
- **No hashtags.**
- **Plain words.** Short over long.
- **Images/screenshots: earn them.** Build the story, land the asset at the end as the payoff. Don't
  open cold on an asset before the reader cares.

## 6. The close — house rule

**Close on a statement. No closing question, no CTA.** `[SOLID]`

This is a deliberate decision and it overrides two other docs (see §9). Some of Lottie's older
high-performing posts end on "Who's next?" or "What do you think?" — we are not doing that. If a
draft drifts toward a closing question, rewrite it into the statement that question was fishing for.
Instead of "Who's next?", end on "The big guys will come knocking."

Two more rules on closers, both from real edits `[SOLID]`:
- **The closer must restate the post's actual thesis.** A line that's thematically adjacent but
  doesn't name what the post argued reads as disjointed, even when the idea is sound.
- **Check a punchy closer is actually true.** "Knowing this channel runs on patience, not proof"
  sounded like an insight; nobody in the source material said it. If in doubt, restate the real
  point plainer rather than reaching for a cleverer one.
- **Cut the "moral of the story" line** when the preceding fact already implies it. Trust the reader.

## 7. Emojis — house rule

**Allowed, sparingly.** Usually as list markers in a short vertical list, or one at the end of a
warm line. Never decorative. If an emoji isn't doing a job, cut it. `[SOLID]` (Lottie's real
best-performing posts use emoji list markers.)

This is the documented exception to the no-emoji default. It overrides the vaguer "LinkedIn:
stricter" line in `../../../rules/writing-rules.md` (see §9).

**No sign-off on a LinkedIn post.** "Lottie x" belongs in email, not here.

## 8. Banned constructions — the hard checklist

These are the tics that make a post read as assembled rather than written. They creep back
constantly, so check every draft against the list. Sources: `copy-learnings.md`, the
`lottie-linkedin` skill, `../../../rules/Anti AI Writing.rtf`.

- **Defining by negation, in every surface form.** The sneakiest one, and a hard rule with no
  exceptions. "I didn't X, I Y" / "not just X" / "No X. Just Y." / "it's not about X, it's about Y"
  / "less X, more Y". The fix is always the same: delete the negation, state the positive thing
  straight. If you catch "not", "didn't", "isn't" or "no" setting up a pivot, rewrite it plain.
- **"not X. It's Y." as a closing line.** Named separately because it keeps coming back
  specifically as the big finish. State the point plainly instead. `[SOLID]`
- **Fragment antithesis:** "Not to X. To Y." Write it as one full sentence.
- **Tidy balanced antithesis** where both halves mirror in length and shape. Break the symmetry:
  keep the point, make the second half longer and concrete.
- **Three short fragments in a row** as a rhythm crutch. Fold them into a flowing sentence.
- **Setup-colon-payoff as a default shape.** "His fix: a simple split." Fine once; three in one
  short post is a tic. Two plain sentences with a full stop instead. `[SOLID]`
- **Dramatic one-beat realisation lines:** "And it landed." "That's the shift." Cut them.
- **Winners-versus-losers pairs** ("the ones struggling" against "the ones flying").
- **"actually" / "actual" as fake emphasis.** Cut every one. `[SOLID]`
- **"what X really means" / "really is"** — same job as "actually". State the topic plainly. `[SOLID]`
- **Mood-setting adverbs** doing emotional work: "quietly", "behind the scenes", "desperately".
  Let the facts carry the feeling. `[SOLID]`
- **Announcing a transition:** "Which is also where his view on X comes in." Cut it and go straight
  in. If the transition needs help, the previous paragraph didn't set up the question. `[SOLID]`
- **Analytical set-up before the point:** "Most bad briefs make the same mistake." Name the thing
  and move. `[SOLID]` Keeps recurring as a labelling tic too, "That's the real difference between X
  and Y", "She's blunt about how far that went", "The habit worth stealing is simple", three in one
  short post. Cut the label sentence, state the content directly.
- **Stock AI quote-intros:** "[X]'s response has stuck with us since", "we haven't stopped thinking
  about". State the point directly. `[SOLID]`
- **"chaos into calm"** — banned permanently, along with any stock AI phrasing of that shape.
- Plus the standing bans: no em dashes, no rule-of-three, no "-ing" analysis tags, no vague
  sourcing, no "In today's fast-paced world", no "This is where X comes in", no "Enter: [thing]".

**The one allowed exception:** a single deliberate reframe per post — the genuine turn ("Revenue,
not profit", "You haven't got a strategy. You've got a document."). Once, as the turn, nowhere else.
And only if it's genuinely sharp: a generic negation-pivot is filler wearing the costume of a
reframe, and doesn't qualify.

## 9. Writing rules that bite hardest here

- **Plain beats clever, even when the clever version isn't wrong.** "They hand over a solution
  dressed up as an instruction" became "You need to be able to tell the creative team the story and
  what's wrong." Flat and talking straight to "you". Only reach for a turn of phrase when it carries
  real insight. `[SOLID]`
- **Quote real words directly.** With the person's actual words available, use quotation marks and
  "as X said," — never launder them into a colon-led paraphrase. `[SOLID]`
- **Active self-agency over passive outcome.** "Stunt your own promotion" beats "you don't get
  promoted". Where the point is about personal responsibility, put the consequence on the reader's
  own action. `[SOLID]`
- **Cutting is the move.** Across every round of edits, Liv removes material rather than adds it,
  even when what got cut wasn't wrong. Default to fewer ideas with more room over covering every
  tip in the source. If you're wondering whether a post needs a fourth or fifth point, it doesn't.
  `[SOLID]`
- **Trim credential asides to one thing, spelled out.** "(she's now Managing Director at Tomorrow
  Brands)", not a stacked CV, and not "MD" for a general audience. `[SOLID]`
- **A post and an email on the same topic need different entry points.** If the post used a story as
  the hook, the email can reference it but needs its own angle. `[SOLID]`
- **Lottie's copy isn't primarily about Lottie.** She's the frame; the subject is the reader, the
  industry, or the content. `[SOLID]`
- **Flag factual conflicts between sources, don't silently resolve them.** A guest's name spelled
  two ways across three sources is a question for Liv, not a judgement call. `[SOLID]`

## 10. Audience

Founders, CEOs, heads of marketing, startup people, FMCG leaders — plus the Up Club core reader
(marketing managers, junior to mid). Assume they're smart, busy, and have already scrolled past a
hundred bland posts today.

A leader wants the spiky take and the trade-off named. A manager wants the same opinion plus more
of the practical steps attached.
