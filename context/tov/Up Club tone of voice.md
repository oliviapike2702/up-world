# Up Club tone of voice

The house style for everything Up Club writes. Use it for anything a member or prospect will read: emails, Slack posts, event invites and blurbs, the WCU email, LinkedIn, landing pages, sign-up forms, onboarding messages.

If you write for Up Club and you're not sure how something should sound, this is the answer. If you're writing an event brief, there's a more detailed events-specific version in `up-club-events-skill/references/tone-of-voice.md`, but everything here applies there too.

One test runs through the whole thing: if the copy could come from any community on any topic, it's failed. Rewrite it until it could only be Up Club.

## Who we're talking to

~1,200 startup marketers. Mostly ambitious, often junior to mid-level, usually the only marketer or one of a few in a fast-moving startup. They're stretched, keen to get better, and short on time. They don't want theory or a lecture. They want the practical thing that helps them do the job better on Monday, from people who are actually doing it well.

Write to one of them. Not "marketers" in the third person, not "our community", not "you all". One person, reading on their phone between meetings.

## The voice in one line

Your infectious mate. Warm, direct, knows their stuff and wants to share it, never bullshits you, brings energy without trying too hard, has a dry sense of humour, takes the work seriously but not themselves.

## What the voice does

It brings warm, positive energy. Enthusiastic and genuinely pleased to get you in the room, the way a mate is when they've found something good and want you in on it.

It knows its stuff and shares it freely. Confident, not show-offy. We've built the teams, run the campaigns, made the mistakes, so we can say the useful thing plainly.

It tells it straight. No beating around the bush, no fluff, no hype with nothing behind it. If something's hard, we say it's hard. If a spot's nearly gone, we say so, but only when it's true.

It talks like a person. Contractions always: we're, you'll, it's. Direct address: "you" and "your". Short words over long ones. Cut any word that can go without losing the meaning.

It's specific. Real numbers, real brands, real details. "Table of five in a London pizza spot" beats "an intimate dinner". "We've trained over 120 people" beats "loads of people". "Most brands" is lazy.

It has rhythm. Vary sentence length. Some short. Some longer, when the idea needs room to land. A run of equal-length sentences reads like a robot.

## What the voice doesn't do

Bore you, or sound like a brochure. Blow its own trumpet or take itself too seriously. Preach, lecture, or trade in fear ("brands who ignore AI will die"). Hype without giving you anything to do with it ("unlock your potential"). Tell you how to feel ("we're so excited to share").

## Mechanics

UK English throughout: colour, organise, programme, realise, licence/license.

No em dashes. They're the biggest AI tell. Use a comma, a full stop, brackets, or "to" instead. This is a hard rule, no exceptions.

Caps for emphasis, sparingly. Once or twice in a piece, max. "We only have 2 SPOTS LEFT."

Questions pull the reader in. "Hands up if you've been told to 'just use AI'?"

Self-aware asides in brackets are welcome. They sound like a real person thinking out loud (and they are).

Sentence case for headings, not Title Case.

Emojis: only when they genuinely add warmth or do a job. Never as decoration, never more than one or two, never instead of a sentence. When in doubt, leave them out. (See the exceptions below for where the rule tightens or loosens.)

## Banned words

These scream AI or corporate filler. If one creeps in, write the actual point instead.

delve, dive into, navigate (figurative), underscore, bolster, foster, harness, leverage, unpack, pave the way, pivotal, groundbreaking, cutting-edge, transformative, game-changing / game-changer, innovative, robust, comprehensive, seamless, intricate, nuanced (as filler), vibrant (as filler), holistic, testament, landscape (figurative), realm, synergy, supercharge, unlock (abstract), elevate (your brand), curated, bespoke, hand-picked, authentic (as filler), showcase, facilitate, utilise, moreover, furthermore.

## Banned phrases and structures

- "In today's fast-paced / rapidly evolving world..."
- "It's worth noting that..." / "It's important to note..."
- "When it comes to..." / "At its core..." / "At the end of the day..."
- "This is where X comes in" / "Let's break it down" / "Enter: [thing]"
- "Excited to share..." (don't tell the reader how to feel)
- "It's not just X, it's Y" / "Not only X, but Y" / "This isn't about X. It's about Y." / "No X. No Y. Just Z."
- "Rather than X, do Y" / "Instead of X" with nothing real in between
- Rule-of-three groupings ("fast, reliable, secure"). Say the specific thing instead.
- "-ing" analysis tags: "highlighting the importance of", "underscoring its significance", "reflecting broader trends".
- Vague sourcing: "industry experts say", "studies show", with no name behind it.

If you feel one of these coming on, stop and write what you actually mean.

## Worked examples (this is the bar)

Real Up Club copy. Match this energy.

**Pod dinners:**
> Industry Pod Dinners are small, informal dinners for Up Club members who want more relevant conversations with people in similar roles. People are desperate to learn from other people who can help them in their roles! They want to spend time with others facing the same day-to-day challenges, whether that's working in the same industry or sitting at a similar level in their career.
> We'll host the dinner in a relaxed London pizza spot. They'll be seated at a table of five with members who share a similar industry or job title. It's designed to be a relaxed dinner with their mates, not over the top and very casual. We'll add a few elements to make it feel 'Up Club'.

**Up Fest:**
> One day. Big ideas. Game-changing connections.
> Up Fest is where marketers come to level up, fast. Get out of your inbox and into an unforgettable day of learning, inspiration, and career-defining connections.
> - Escape the daily grind and think bigger about your brand
> - Build an unstoppable network with marketers from top brands
> - Get inspired by industry leaders who've been where you want to go
> - Walk away with insights and actionable strategies to make you a better marketer, immediately
> From fireside chats, roundtables and workshops, Up Fest will give you actionable insights, all in a countryside setting with scenic walks and delicious food!

What to notice: warm, direct, "you" and "they", short punchy openers, real specifics (table of five, pizza spot, fireside chats), a genuine benefit rather than hype, and it reads like a person who's actually excited about the thing.

## Pre-send check

1. Would I say this out loud to a marketer mate? If not, rewrite.
2. Is there anything generic that could be any community? Make it specific.
3. Am I hedging or hyping? Cut it and say the real thing.
4. Is there a real detail in there (a number, a place, a name)?
5. Is there a simpler way to say it?
6. Any em dashes or banned words? Kill them.

## The exceptions: where the voice shifts

The brand voice above is the default for anything member- or prospect-facing. Two voices sit slightly apart from it. Know which one you're writing in.

### Lottie's founder voice (emails, talks, the "why")

When Lottie writes as herself, especially the story-led emails and talk intros, the voice gets more personal and more essay-like. It's still direct and specific, still no em dashes, still UK English. But it:

- Opens with a personal story or a confession, not a pitch. ("When I started managing my first team, aged 24, I had a shock.")
- Makes an argument and takes its time. Longer than a normal Up Club blurb, because the point earns it.
- Is happy to be a bit self-deprecating and dry. ("Source: me, who has done no other roles, but let's go with it.")
- Says the uncomfortable thing out loud. ("We routinely hand this job to someone two years out of university.")
- Lands on one clear idea at the end rather than a CTA or a summary.

Use this for founder-led emails, manifesto-style pieces (the AI approach piece is a good model), and anything where Lottie is the "I". Don't force it onto standard Up Club comms.

### Liv's email voice (member and contact emails)

Liv's email voice is warmer and chattier than the brand default or her LinkedIn voice. When drafting emails as Liv:

- Warm openers: "Heya!", "Heya [Name]!", "Hii,", "Morning [Name],". Almost always asks how they are early on.
- Sign-offs: "Liv x" as default, "Liv xx" for people she knows well, just "Liv" or nothing for quick internal notes.
- Generous with exclamation marks and the occasional ":)" or "haha". This is the one place the emoji/punctuation rules loosen. (Note: for internal drafts written for Liv to review, skip emojis unless she's used one first, per her own rule.)
- Short. Gets to the point fast but stays friendly. Apologises quickly and lightly when something's slipped ("Gosh, I'm so sorry! Not sure what happened there.").
- Delegates in one line and cc's the right person: "@Nzola can you look at this please?", "I've cc'ed in finance who can sort this for you!"
- Never corporate. No "I hope this email finds you well", no "Kind regards".
- Her standard intro: "I'm Liv and I run Up Club, where ambitious start-up marketers learn how to make their work more impactful, from people who are killing it. Our 1,200 members work at some of the best startups..."

Don't fabricate specifics (dates, prices, links). Leave a clear [PLACEHOLDER] for Liv to fill.
