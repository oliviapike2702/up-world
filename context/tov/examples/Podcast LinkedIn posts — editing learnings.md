# Podcast LinkedIn posts — editing learnings

A running before/after teaching doc, same idea as `Email 4 — TOV comparison.md`. Built from Liv's
own edit of the Ruth Fittock (BMBC podcast ep. 2) LinkedIn post draft, July 2026. Add to this file
whenever a real edit teaches a generalisable lesson, don't just log every change.

## Banned phrases (add to the standing list alongside "chaos into calm")
- "[X]'s response has stuck with us since"
- "we haven't stopped thinking about [since]"

Both are stock AI transition phrases for introducing a quote or takeaway. State the point directly
instead: "Lottie's response:" or just cut straight to the thing that stuck. Also logged in the
`writing-avoid-ai-cliches` memory.

## Lessons from Liv's edit (Ruth Fittock post)

**1. More white space, shorter paragraph units than I default to.**
I bunch two or three sentences into a paragraph. Liv breaks almost every sentence onto its own
line, even ones that read fine bunched. LinkedIn scans better broken up. Even in Up World "we"
voice (not just Lottie's personal voice), err toward more line breaks than feels natural in prose.

**2. Active self-agency over passive outcome.**
- Mine: "you don't get promoted."
- Liv's: "stunt your own promotion."
Hers puts the consequence on the reader's own action rather than describing something that
happens to them. Look for passive "X doesn't happen to you" phrasing and rewrite as active
"you do/don't do X" wherever the point is about personal responsibility.

**3. Quote real words directly, don't paraphrase them into indirect speech.**
- Mine: "Lottie's response: a huge shortcut to seniority is..."  (paraphrase dressed with a colon)
- Liv's: 'As Lottie said, "a huge shortcut to seniority is..."' (an actual attributed quote)
When we have someone's real words from a transcript, quote them in quotation marks with "as X
said," rather than laundering them into a colon-led paraphrase. Matches the house rule of
grounding claims in real quotes, not summaries of them.

**4. Turn a framework into a real list, not a run-on sentence.**
- Mine: "who you're talking to, what you're saying, why the company exists, then how you talk
  about it" — one sentence.
- Liv's: four stacked lines, one per element.
Whenever the source material is a named framework (a "who/what/how/why", a numbered model, a
before/after), break it into actual visual list lines. It signals "this is a framework" and is
far easier to scan than parsing it out of prose.

**5. Cut the explanatory "moral of the story" line when the fact already implies it.**
I added, after the £100m stat: "That's not a reason to wait for someone else to write one. It's
permission to do it yourself." Liv cut it entirely. If the preceding fact already carries the
implication, trust the reader — don't spell out the moral.

**6. Trim a credential aside to one thing, and spell it out.**
- Mine: "(MD at Tomorrow Brands, after Vitamin Water and Popchips)"
- Liv's: "(she's now Managing Director at Tomorrow Brands)"
Pick the single most relevant credential rather than stacking the whole CV into a parenthetical,
and spell out abbreviations like "MD" for a general LinkedIn audience rather than assuming they'll
parse it instantly.

## Lessons from Liv's edit (David Fryman post)

**7. Plain beats clever, even when the clever version isn't wrong.**
- Mine: "they hand over a solution dressed up as an instruction"
- Liv's: "You need to be able to tell the creative team the story and what's wrong."
"Dressed up as" is a writerly flourish, a small metaphor doing a job a flat sentence could do just
as well. Liv's version is plainer and, crucially, talks straight to "you" as an instruction rather
than describing the mistake in the abstract. Prefer the flat, direct version by default; only
reach for a turn of phrase when it's carrying real insight, not just style.

**8. Skip the analytical set-up, name the skill and move.**
- Mine: "Most bad briefs make the same mistake." (framing the point as a pattern before stating it)
- Liv's: "Being able to write a killer brief is a core skill which you need in brand management."
  (states the claim directly, no framing sentence first)
Don't build a little "here's the common mistake" preamble before making the point. Say the thing.

**9. Cutting is the consistent move, round after round, even when the cut material was solid.**
Across every edit of this post (the senior-stakeholder story, the relationship/one-way-door bit,
the "spend time to save time" closer, and now the "ask to see the agency's brief" paragraph), Liv's
edits removed material rather than added it, even when what got cut wasn't wrong or badly written.
When drafting, default to fewer ideas covered with more room to breathe over covering every
available tip from the source material. If in doubt about whether a post needs a fourth or fifth
point, it probably doesn't.

**10. Don't silently resolve a factual discrepancy between sources, flag it.**
Liv's own message spelled a guest's name differently ("Frymann") than the transcript and every
prior draft ("Fryman") — and a third source (the BMBC_2026.ics cohort calendar) also said
"Frymann", meaning the sources disagreed with each other, not just with the draft. Correct move:
flag the conflict explicitly and ask, rather than picking one silently (even the "more common in
the source material" one) or assuming the most recent message is authoritative by default.

## How to use this
