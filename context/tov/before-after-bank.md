## 1. Job spec intro — negation-pivot, repetition, intensifiers

**Context:** Intro paragraph of a replacement job spec. Lottie herself flagged the first pass as
sounding AI, before Liv's edit.

**Rejected:**
> You'll take on a community that 1,200 marketers pay to be part of, and a set of courses people finish and then recommend to their friends. We're incredibly proud of everything we do today and have remarkably high NPS and retention. But, we also believe neither is anywhere near as big or as good as it could be, and we want someone to take that personally! Up World is four people, nothing sits in a queue waiting for sign-off. You can have an idea on a Monday and be running it in front of members in a month, with a founder who'll back a good idea quickly. You'll have room to change things, including the things we're currently proud of. We'd rather you brought us an idea that scares us slightly than one we've already had. You'll be the person who decides how much of this job a machine should do. We're four people running something that behaves like a much bigger company, and that only works because we automate hard.

**Fixed:**
> You'll take on a community that 1,200 marketers pay to be part of, and courses people finish and then recommend to their friends. We're proud of what we've built. Retention is high and so is our NPS. But neither is close to as big or as good as it could be, and we want someone to take that personally! Up World is four people, so there's no approval chain to get through. Have an idea on a Monday and you could be running it in front of members inside the month, and Lottie will back a good one fast. You'll have room to change things, including the things we like. Bring us something we haven't thought of, even if it makes us a bit nervous. You'll also decide how much of this job a person needs to do at all. A team this small can only run something this big by automating hard, and that's a big part of the job here.

**Fed into:** the "we'd rather X than Y" preference-contrast ban; "repetition of a key number or
word reads as machine-assembled" (1,200 repeated four times, "proud"/"four people" twice each);
the "incredibly"/"remarkably" intensifier extension.

## 2. Job spec — trait-name vs. behaviour

**Rejected:**
> We're looking for someone who has a real passion for bringing people together, loves meeting IRL, can bring bold and brave ideas to the team and is passionate for automating and working smarter not harder.

**Fixed:**
> The person we're after gets a kick out of putting people in a room together and would sooner spend an afternoon building the automation than do the same task forty times.

**Liv's reaction:** "this is great, I do love this line."

**Fed into:** "Name the behaviour, not the virtue" and the openers extension ("Relationship
builder, you know how to work a room" reads as filler; a recognisable behaviour doesn't).

## 6. Ruth Fittock LinkedIn post — the fullest before/after in the bank

**Context:** Recap post from the Brand Manager Bootcamp podcast episode with Ruth Fittock
(now Managing Director at Tomorrow Brands).

**Rejected `[THIN]`** (reconstructed from the diff, not pasted whole in the session — treat exact
wording as approximate):
> Ruth Fittock spent eight years at Popchips and became the person everyone asked when something was on or off brand. Lottie's response: a huge shortcut to seniority is asking yourself how you codify what you know, so the culture holds it, not just you. Brand strategy, Ruth says, is who you're talking to, what you're saying, why the company exists, then how you talk about it. Most brands don't even have that written down properly...

**Fixed (fully verbatim, pasted by Liv in-session):**
> Ruth Fittock spent eight years at Popchips. By the end, she says she felt like "an old lady in the corner in a rocking chair."
>
> People would just come to her: ask Ruth, ask Ruth. She'd built such an instinct for what was on and off brand that she'd become the answer to everything.
>
> Although it sounds like a compliment, it's actually a warning.
>
> We had Ruth on the Brand Manager Bootcamp podcast (she's now Managing Director at Tomorrow Brands), and she and Lottie talked about what happens when you become the only person who holds the brand in your head.
>
> If it's not written down, you get stuck approving things forever and stunt your own promotion.
>
> As Lottie said "a huge shortcut to seniority is asking yourself how you codify what you know, so the culture holds it, not just you"
>
> It's the same problem underneath everything else they talked about.
>
> Brand strategy, Ruth says, is just your who, what, how, and why.
>
> Who you're talking to / What you're saying / Why the company exists / How you talk about it.
>
> If you try skip straight to the how, the ads, the content, without the rest written down first, and nothing lands, because nobody's actually agreed what you're saying yet.
>
> Most brands don't even have that written down properly.
>
> Ruth's worked on brands turning over £100 million without a real strategy document.
>
> Same goes for targets. Two or three, maximum, never five or seven...
>
> None of it needs a big deck or an expensive agency either.
>
> Ruth's old tone of voice guidelines at Popchips were a single page: we are, we are not, with a line down the middle.

**Liv's own diagnostic, verbatim:** "This is how I've edited. Any learnings you can take from the
OG to this?" — six named lessons: more white space/shorter lines; active self-agency ("stunt your
own promotion" not "you don't get promoted"); quote Lottie directly, don't paraphrase-with-colon;
present a framework as a real stacked list, not a run-on sentence; cut the explanatory wrap-up
line; trim the credential list to one thing.

## 11. Brand manager bullet points (likely the "brand manager panel recap email, Aug 2026" source)

**Rejected:**
> 1. Fight for the vision, every single day. Don't let campaign requests and sign-off chains become the whole job. Someone has to hold the line on what the brand actually stands for, that's you.
> 2. Put the wildest idea on the page first. Before you refine anything, write down the version with no budget, no legal, no limits. Cut it down from there, never the other way round.
> 3. Steal from outside your category. Once a month, look at a brand that has nothing to do with yours and ask what you'd take from it. Your competitors are the last place left to look for a fresh idea.
> 4. Learn exactly how your business makes money. Sit down with finance or your commercial lead and ask them to walk you through the P&L. If you can't explain where growth comes from in five years, you're managing a brand, not the business.
> 5. Interrogate the data that agrees with you. The moment a number confirms what you already believed, go and ask why, not whether. That's where the real insight is hiding.
> 6. Talk to a real customer every single week. Get in a room with one, get them on a call. If you can't remember the last time you did it, that's the gap to close first.

**Fixed (Lottie's own edit):**
> Get very good at influencing. If the team are asking you questions or the execution isn't what you hoped, then you need to educate better. Your job is to teach everyone how the brand shows up, so they never have to ask.
>
> 1. Always think big first. Put the wildest idea on the page and don't worry about the practicalities. Before you refine anything, write down the version with no budget, no legal, no limits. Cut it down from there, never the other way round.
> 2. Stay inspired. Once a month, look at a brand that has nothing to do with yours and ask what you'd take from it. You need to steal from outside your category and take your creative brain very seriously.
> 3. Learn exactly how your business makes money. Sit down with finance or your commercial lead and ask them to walk you through the P&L.
> 4. Interrogate the data that agrees with you. The moment a number confirms what you already believed, go and ask why, not whether. That's where the real insight is hiding.
> 5. Talk to a real customer every single week. Get in a room with one, get them on a call, even buy them a pint! If you can't remember the last time you did it, that's the gap to close first.

**What changed:** the didactic "someone has to hold the line... that's you" direct-address
moralising got cut for a plainer influencing/educating frame up top. A permission clause ("don't
worry about the practicalities") got added before the boldest ask. A loose warm beat ("even buy
them a pint!") got added to the last bullet. The over-clever "your competitors are the last place
left to look" line got cut outright — a soundbite that wasn't earning its place.

**Fed into:** "Add a permission clause where the instruction feels risky or indulgent"; "Break the
instructional rhythm with one loose, warm beat"; "Bold lead-ins are verb-first imperatives, never
noun-phrase descriptors."
