# Copy learnings

Empirical rules derived from Liv's edits to real Up World copy. Load alongside the TOV docs for any writing task.

Each rule is actionable and sourced from a real edit — not aspiration, but pattern.

Organised by scope: **General** applies everywhere, **Email** and **LinkedIn** are channel-specific.
When adding a new rule, put it under the narrowest section it genuinely belongs to — if in doubt,
General.

---

# Process: pulling takeaways/bullet points from a transcript

Checklist for turning a raw transcript (panel, talk, podcast) into a bulleted list of takeaways in
Lottie's voice. Built from the brand manager panel recap email, Aug 2026, comparing multiple AI
drafts against Lottie's own edit line by line.

1. **Extract the plainest point a speaker made, not the rhetorical one.** When someone says "I don't
   want to skip X, but...", that's a caveat flagging X in passing, not necessarily the headline.
   Look at what the discussion actually spent the most concrete time on, not what got flagged as
   important in an aside.
2. **Each bullet needs a symptom, a real cause, and a fix, not a virtue name.** "Have a vision" or
   "be inspired" names a quality, it gives the reader nothing to do. Give them a situation they'll
   recognise, the actual cause, and the fix. Bullets shaped this way survived Lottie's edit
   untouched, bullets shaped as virtue names got rewritten every round.
3. **Add a permission clause where the instruction feels risky or indulgent.** "Don't worry about
   the practicalities" isn't new information, it removes the reader's anxiety before asking them to
   do something bold. Any instruction asking someone to be reckless, wasteful, or over-ambitious
   needs a line giving them cover to actually do it.
4. **Break the instructional rhythm with one loose, warm beat.** An exclamation mark, an aside like
   "even buy them a pint", one moment of looseness in an otherwise tight list stops it reading like
   a manual.
5. **Bold lead-ins are verb-first imperatives, never noun-phrase descriptors.** "Fight for the
   vision" not "Vision". "Talk to a real customer" not "Customer proximity".
6. **Never define by negation, and re-check the whole draft after fixing it once.** The "not X, it's
   Y" pattern hides in more than one line of the same draft even after being caught and fixed once.
   Scan every bullet against the rule, not just the one that got flagged.
7. **When a draft and the real edit diverge and the gap isn't obvious, ask for one real line before
   generating more rounds.** Guessing again rarely closes the gap; one genuine example, however
   rough, lets you pattern-match the actual register instead of inventing a "punchier" version.

# General (all Up World and Lottie copy)

## Borrowed real anecdotes, not just her own CV, can carry a point (candidate, untested in written copy)

**Rule:** The existing rule says she argues from her own CV, never borrowed authority. True for
credentials, but not the whole picture: she also uses other people's real, specific anecdotes (a
friend's Netflix story about training time and written-comms quality) to make a broader industry
point concrete. The line to hold: the anecdote must be genuinely real and specific, never invented
or generalised into a composite "someone told me once" story.
**Applies to:** Lottie copy making an industry-level claim.
**Source:** "Why you shouldn't be sleeping on AI" Up Talk transcript, Aug 2026.

---

## Deflate overthinking with a mocking, everyday metaphor (candidate, untested in written copy)

**Rule:** Distinct from her usual grounding metaphors (Toast catalogue, tiler), this move
specifically targets procrastination or overthinking by making it sound a bit silly. "I think it's
the equivalent of me saying I'm going to start jogging round the park, but first tell me what gels
I need and what trainers to buy... just get out the door." Use when the point is "stop overthinking
and start," not when illustrating a neutral concept.
**Applies to:** Lottie copy addressing procrastination or excessive prep before starting something.
**Source:** "Why you shouldn't be sleeping on AI" Up Talk transcript, Aug 2026.

---

## Validate the smart version of an objection, dismiss only the naive one (candidate, untested in written copy)

**Rule:** Stronger and more credible than blanket dismissal of pushback. On AI, she draws a clear
line: someone who is genuinely informed and still chooses not to use it in a given workflow gets
real respect as an "informed no." Someone who opts out from fear or a lack of understanding gets
pushed back on. This shows she's actually engaged with the counter-argument rather than
strawmanning all disagreement, and it's a sharper way to take a side than dismissing everyone who
disagrees. This is the strongest of the three new patterns from this transcript — worth testing
first in a written draft that takes a controversial stance.
**Applies to:** All Lottie copy taking a controversial or "controlled controversy" position.
**Source:** "Why you shouldn't be sleeping on AI" Up Talk transcript, Aug 2026.

---

## Personal-stakes closer: tie the opinion to why she personally cares (candidate, untested in written copy)

**Rule:** In her own spoken talks, Lottie closes a strong opinion by naming why it matters to her
personally, not just stating the opinion. "It's quite shocking how many people have never had any
investment in their training... I feel really strongly about it, which is a lot of why I do what I
do." This is a real spoken pattern, not yet confirmed in edited written copy — treat as a
hypothesis to try on a closing line, not a settled rule.
**Applies to:** Lottie sales/story emails, closing lines.
**Source:** "How To Be a Great Brand Manager" Up Talk transcript, Aug 2026.

---

## Overdescriptive scene-setting reads as filler, even when true

**Rule:** A real physical detail from the background bank ("stood on the platform at King's Cross
about to get a train") doesn't earn its place just because it's factually accurate. If the emotional
beat of the story is already clear without it, cut the scene-setting rather than trimming it to a
shorter version of the same detail.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy.
**Source:** "Why good marketers get stuck at manager level" event blurb and email, Aug 2026 — Liv
flagged the King's Cross platform detail as pointless in both the blurb and the email draft.

---

## "Here's my honest opinion on..." is itself an AI tell

**Rule:** Announcing that an opinion is coming, or flagging it as "honest," is throat-clearing —
same family as announcing a transition. State the opinion directly instead. A short causal
one-liner ("You're too good at your current job. That's why you're not getting the next one.")
lands harder than framing it as "here's my honest opinion on why you're not getting promoted."
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy, especially opinion/stance lines.
**Source:** "Why you're not getting promoted" email draft, Aug 2026.

---

## "Quietly" and similar hedging language

**Rule:** Never use "quietly", "behind the scenes", or similar phrases that try to create intrigue around something being done. It reads as affected and try-hard.
**Applies to:** All Up World copy
**Source:** Email 5 draft, Jul 2026

---

## Lottie's voice — the "me me me" trap

**Rule:** Lottie's emails should not be primarily about Lottie. Her voice is self-deprecating and personal, but the subject matter should be about the reader, the industry, or the content — with Lottie as the frame. If a draft is mostly about what Lottie has done/thinks, rebalance toward what the reader gets.
**Applies to:** All Lottie-voice copy
**Source:** Feedback on emails 2–6, Jul 2026

---

## Openers — name the specific behaviour, not the abstract problem

**Rule:** Openers hit harder when the reader recognises a real moment, not a general pattern. "They copy one they saw their manager make" lands better than "they copy what they've seen before." The more specific and recognisable the behaviour, the more the reader feels seen.
**Applies to:** All Up World copy
**Source:** Email 5 edit, Jul 2026

**Extension (Aug 2026):** This applies to requirement and trait lists too, not just openers. "Relationship
builder, you know how to work a room" and "great at making decisions" are assertions nobody
self-selects out of, and filler sitting next to strong lines makes the strong lines look like filler as
well. Describe something the person would recognise themselves doing instead ("you've hosted the event,
chased the RSVPs, and fixed the thing that broke ten minutes before it started").
**Source:** Replacement job spec, Aug 2026.

---

## Short declarative sentences to land the point of view

**Rule:** After setting up the problem, land the conclusion in short sentences with full stops. "It's not complicated. But it does need to be taught." No hedging, no "however", no comma chains. This is the moment Lottie takes a side — let it land cleanly.
**Applies to:** All Lottie copy
**Source:** Email 5 edit, Jul 2026

---

## Reframe the common excuse

**Rule:** When naming an industry misconception, use "When the excuse is X, more often than not, it's Y." Sharper than passive constructions like "why X is often actually Y." Positions Lottie as someone who sees through the received wisdom.
**Applies to:** All Lottie sales copy
**Source:** Email 5 edit, Jul 2026

---

## Email vs LinkedIn — distinct angles required

**Rule:** If a LinkedIn post and an email cover the same topic, they must use different entry points. If the post used a story as the hook, the email cannot open with the same story. The email can reference it, but needs its own angle (a broader truth, a different question, a fresh problem).
**Applies to:** All Up World email and social
**Source:** Email 5 — LinkedIn post used the dinosaur brief analogy as the hook; email needed a different opener

---

## Never use "not X. It's Y." as a closing line (or anywhere)

**Rule:** Hard rule, no exceptions: never write a sentence pair in the shape "That's not a job
description. It's the whole team's job." This is the negation-contrast pivot ("not X, it's Y")
already banned elsewhere in TOV, but it keeps slipping back in specifically as a *closing line* for
LinkedIn posts, where it reads as a forced, AI-sounding "big finish." State the point plainly
instead: "At OkiShield, everyone owns the brand."
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy, especially post/email closing lines.
**Source:** Dhruvin Patel LinkedIn post draft, Jul 2026.

---

## "Actually" as fake emphasis

**Rule:** Cut "actually" and "actual" wherever they're added purely for emphasis rather than real
contrast ("what founders actually want", "a marketer a founder actually trusts"). It's a default AI
crutch for sounding punchy without saying anything sharper. State the claim plainly without it.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy, especially titles and headlines.
**Source:** Dhruvin Patel podcast title options, Jul 2026 — used three times across five title
suggestions in one go, despite already being a documented banned word elsewhere in TOV.

---

## Setup-colon-payoff is a crutch, not a style

**Rule:** Stop reaching for "[setup]: [payoff]" as a default sentence shape ("His fix: a simple
split", "His own test is blunt: if you're on the fence..."). Used once in a piece it's fine; used
three times in one short post it reads as a tic, the same rhythm repeated instead of varied
sentence structure. Write it as two plain sentences with a full stop instead.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy.
**Source:** Dhruvin Patel LinkedIn post draft, Jul 2026 — three colon-setups in one ~1,650 char post.

---

## Don't announce a transition, just make it

**Rule:** Never write a sentence whose only job is to signal "now I'm moving to the next point"
("Which is also where his view on X comes in"). Cut it and go straight into the content. If the
transition needs help, that's a sign the previous paragraph didn't set up the question the next
one answers, fix that instead of bridging with meta-commentary.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy.
**Source:** Dhruvin Patel LinkedIn post draft, Jul 2026.

---

## A closer must restate the post's actual thesis, not drift into a new (even related) idea

**Rule:** The final line needs to explicitly connect back to what the post has been arguing, not
introduce a fresh, thematically-adjacent idea and call it a landing. A closer that's "related" but
doesn't name the actual thesis reads as disjointed, even when the idea itself is sound.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy, especially post/email closing lines.
**Source:** Dhruvin Patel LinkedIn post draft, Jul 2026 — "nobody told that team to go looking,
they'd earned the trust" was cut for feeling disconnected, even though it wasn't wrong on its own.

---

## Check a punchy-sounding closer is actually true before using it

**Rule:** A line can sound sharp and still be invented. "Knowing this channel runs on patience, not
proof" reads like a real insight, but nobody in the source material contrasted patience against
proof, that pairing came from nowhere. Before using a snappy contrast or soundbite as a closer, check
it restates something actually said, rather than inventing a new-sounding claim just because the
sentence shape works. If in doubt, restate the real point plainer rather than reach for a cleverer
one.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy.
**Source:** Charlotte Griffiths LinkedIn post draft, Jul 2026 — Liv caught "patience, not proof" as
not actually true to what Charlotte or Lottie said, twice, before it got fixed.

---

## "Really means" / "really is" as a fake-weight intensifier

**Rule:** Titles and headlines reaching for "what X really means" or "what X really is" are doing
the same job as "actually", dressing up a plain claim to sound more dramatic or revelatory than it
is. State the topic plainly instead ("What being a brand manager takes" beats "What being a brand
manager really means").
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy, especially titles and headlines.
**Source:** Lizzie Staiano podcast title options, Jul 2026.

---

## "Real" as a fake-weight intensifier

**Rule:** Cut "the real job", "what it really takes", "the real reason". Same crutch as
"actually" and "really means", already banned above: it dresses up a plain claim to sound
revelatory. "The job is holding the brand's vision in one hand" is stronger than "the real job
is". Watch for it creeping back in via edits, not just first drafts.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy.
**Source:** Up Talk email, Aug 2026 — Liv flagged "the real job", then "really takes" appeared in
her own replacement line and needed cutting again.

**Extension (Aug 2026):** Same family, same rule: cut "incredibly", "remarkably" and "amazing".
They almost always prop up a claim that stands up better without them. "Our NPS is high" beats
"we have remarkably high NPS", and "we're proud of what we've built" beats "we're incredibly proud
of everything we do". If the claim needs an intensifier to land, the claim is the problem.
**Source:** Replacement job spec, Aug 2026 — Lottie flagged the intro paragraph as sounding AI.

---

## Don't open a sentence with "Because"

**Rule:** Never start a sentence with "Because". It reads as a fragment bolted onto the previous
thought. Either join it to the sentence before with a comma, or restate the subject and make it
stand alone.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy.
**Source:** Up Talk email, Aug 2026.

---

## Use the real phrase that already exists before inventing a cleverer one

**Rule:** When the source material, the brief, or Liv's own message already contains a specific,
human phrase, use it. Don't generate a smarter-sounding alternative on top of it. The invented
version reads as AI precisely because it is reaching for cleverness where the real phrase was
reaching for a meaning. Liv's "come and gossip about brand management" beat every subject line
generated around it.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy, especially subject lines, headlines and closers.
**Source:** Up Talk email, Aug 2026 — Liv rejected two full rounds of suggestions as "so AI",
then supplied the winning line herself out of a sentence already sitting in the email body.

---

## The "we'd rather X than Y" preference contrast

**Rule:** Don't build a point out of a stated preference between two options ("We'd rather you
brought us an idea that scares us slightly than one we've already had"). It's the same
symmetrical-contrast family as the banned "not X, it's Y" and the "most people do X, almost nobody
does Y" pattern, but it slips past the guard disguised as generosity or an invitation. Write the
plain version: "Bring us something we haven't thought of, even if it makes us a bit nervous."
Watch for its cousin too: the slogan-shaped sentence whose only content is its rhythm ("You'll be
the person who decides how much of this job a machine should do").
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy.
**Source:** Replacement job spec, Aug 2026 — Lottie flagged the intro paragraph as sounding AI, and
these two lines were the main offenders.

---

## Repetition of a key number or word reads as machine-assembled

**Rule:** A strong stat loses force every time it's repeated, and repeating a distinctive word or
phrase within a few sentences reads as generated rather than written. Use the headline number once
near the top and once where it does real work, then stop. If a word has to reappear, split the
paragraph so the repetition has some distance. Check for this specifically in long documents built
up over several passes, where each pass adds the number again without seeing the others.
**Applies to:** All Up World copy, especially long-form documents edited in passes.
**Source:** Replacement job spec, Aug 2026 — "1,200 members" appeared four times across the doc, and
"proud" and "four people" twice each inside one paragraph.

# Email

## Sales email openers

**Rule:** Never open a Lottie sales email with a pleasantry ("Hope you're having a good week", "Hey everyone"). Open with a statement, a bold claim, or a controversial truth.
**Applies to:** All Lottie and Up World sales/launch emails
**Source:** Email 5 draft, Jul 2026

---

## Sales email vs member email register

**Rule:** Warm openers ("Heya", "hope your week's going well") belong in member emails — emails to existing Up Club members. Sales emails need a punchy opener. The distinction is relationship: member = already in the room; sales = convincing someone to come in.
**Applies to:** All Up World email
**Source:** Email 5 feedback, Jul 2026

---

## Header text — not generic descriptors

**Rule:** Email header text (the text overlay on the Canva graphic) must be a claim or provocation, not a descriptor. "8 episodes, 8 marketers" and "The podcast that comes with the course" are descriptors — they say what the thing is, not why it matters. Header text should make someone feel something.
**Applies to:** All BMBC and Up World campaign email headers
**Source:** Email 5 feedback, Jul 2026

---

## Email length

**Rule:** Lottie's sales emails should be substantive — not padded, but properly developed. Short drafts that don't build the argument fully are not in TOV. The test: does each paragraph add something, or is it filling space? Cut padding; don't mistake length for depth.
**Applies to:** All Lottie sales emails
**Source:** Emails 2–6 first drafts flagged as "weirdly short and not in Lottie's TOV"

---

## Subject lines — specific beats vague

**Rule:** Specific social proof in subject lines dramatically outperforms vague educational angles. "Three promotions since the last cohort" (65.9% open rate) vs "It's not that you lack confidence" (28.6%). When there's a real number, name, or result — use it in the subject.
**Applies to:** All Up World sales email subject lines
**Source:** BMBC 2026 campaign data — Email 4 vs Email 2

---

## Check the angle hasn't just been used before proposing subject lines

**Rule:** Before offering subject lines, pull the last ~45 sent campaigns
(`GET /campaigns?status=sent&sort_field=send_time&sort_dir=DESC`) with their subject lines, open
and click rates, and check the angle isn't a repeat. Up World sends often enough that a strong
argument gets reused within weeks without anyone noticing.
**Applies to:** All Up World email subject lines.
**Source:** Up Talk email, Aug 2026 — "nobody trained your brand manager" had already run twice in
a month ("P&G trained their brand managers. Most startups just hired one." 27 Jul, 49.4% open /
4.0% click; "Who taught your brand manager how to do their job?" 7 Jul, 38.7% / 2.2%) and a third
version was about to go out. Liv spotted it, not me.

---

## What actually wins in Up World subject lines: concrete and slightly odd

**Rule:** The top performers name a specific, faintly embarrassing, real thing. Not clever, not
abstract, not a rhetorical question. Benchmarks from the Jun–Jul 2026 sends: "How to explain
creative briefs to a 10-year-old" 69.7% · "Three promotions since the last cohort" 67.0% ·
"The Tesco listing is the easy part." 52.9%. Compare "It's not that you lack confidence" at 29.2%.
**Applies to:** All Up World email subject lines.
**Source:** Mailchimp send history pulled 3 Aug 2026.

---

## Headers and subject lines — provoke, don't describe

**Rule:** Headers and subject lines should name what the reader is missing or hasn't been given, not describe the product or its contents. "You haven't been taught the skills to be a great brand manager" provokes. "8 episodes, 8 marketers" describes. Provocation wins.
**Applies to:** All Up World email headers and subject lines
**Source:** Email 5 edit, Jul 2026

---

## "That lands" — never use

**Rule:** Never write "feedback that lands", "copy that lands", "a message that lands" or any variant. It's an AI-tell filler phrase that adds nothing. Say what the thing does instead, or cut it.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy
**Source:** Liv, Aug 2026

---

## Button copy — no arrows

**Rule:** Never include arrows (→ or >) in CTA button text. "Sign up now" not "Sign up now →". Arrows look automated and cheap.
**Applies to:** All Up World email and landing page buttons
**Source:** Email 5 feedback, Jul 2026

---

## Speaker/person links in email — bold, brand colour, underlined

**Rule:** When hyperlinking a person's name in email body copy, always make it bold, pink (`#ea526d`), and underlined. Plain hyperlinks with no visual treatment are invisible — readers won't know they can click.
**Applies to:** All Up World emails with speaker or guest name mentions
**Source:** Email 5 build, Jul 2026

---

## First course name mention — bold hyperlink to course page

**Rule:** The first mention of a course name in the email body should be bold and hyperlinked to the course sign-up page. Subsequent mentions can be plain text.
**Applies to:** All Up World course sales emails
**Source:** Email 5 build, Jul 2026

# LinkedIn

## A hook doesn't always need an invented scene, a strong real quote can lead directly

**Rule:** Don't assume every post needs a manufactured cold-open anecdote. Sometimes the strongest
real quote from the transcript, leading straight in, creates its own curiosity gap ("who says this,
and why") without needing a scene built in front of it. Check whether the quote alone earns the
opening spot before adding a story purely for hook value.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie LinkedIn/podcast posts.
**Source:** Dhruvin Patel LinkedIn post draft, Jul 2026 — added an HR-director anecdote as the hook,
Liv cut it and led with the quote directly instead.

---

## The manufactured two-part contrast ("most people do X, almost nobody does Y")

**Rule:** Don't build a point out of a symmetrical contrast between what most people do and what
almost nobody does. It has the rhythm of an insight without containing one, and it's the same
negation-pivot family as the banned "not X, it's Y". Name the thing that actually happens instead,
in one sentence, with the real behaviour in it.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy.
**Source:** TMA Sept 2026 launch email draft, Jul 2026 — Liv flagged "Most marketers answer with a
project. Almost nobody answers with a plan for themselves" as feeling AI-y.

---

## "And here's the..." as a transition

**Rule:** Never open a paragraph with "And here's the bit that...", "And here's the thing", "Here's
what most people miss" or any variant. It's the announce-a-transition problem in its most common
disguise, and it flags the next point as profound before making it. Delete the framing and start
with the point itself.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy.
**Source:** TMA Sept 2026 email 2 draft, Jul 2026 — Liv called it "AI slop of 'and here's the'".
Note this duplicates the existing "Don't announce a transition, just make it" rule, which shows
the general rule wasn't enough on its own to stop the specific phrasing.

---

## Don't stack short sentences

**Rule:** Short declaratives land a point of view, but they only work sparingly. Three or four in a
row reads as a tic rather than emphasis, and the effect inverts. Vary the length: let one long
flowing sentence carry the detail, then land the conclusion short. If two consecutive sentences are
both under about eight words, one of them should probably be folded into its neighbour.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy.
**Source:** TMA Sept 2026 email 2 draft, Jul 2026 — Liv flagged "My team hadn't. The gulf, it turned
out, was huge, and I had no real idea how to close it." as overuse of short sentences. Sits
alongside the existing "short declarative sentences" rule, which this bounds rather than contradicts.

---

## One real example beats several rounds of invented ones

**Rule:** When a draft keeps missing Liv's ear after 2-3 rounds, stop generating more variations
and ask her to write one line herself, however rough. A single real example is worth more than
further guessing, because it lets you pattern-match the actual rhythm and register instead of
inventing a "punchier" version of your own. This extends the existing "use the real phrase that
already exists" rule from wording specifically to structure and rhythm.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy, especially iterative rounds of feedback.
**Source:** Brand manager panel recap email, Aug 2026 — four rounds of AI-generated bullet
headers all missed, one line from Liv ("You need to be fighting for the brand vision at all
times") unlocked the right register immediately.

---

## Name the behaviour, not the virtue

**Rule:** A bullet or header that names a quality ("have a vision", "the brand managers who stand
out") reads as aspirational, not actionable. Pair the punchy instruction with a concrete
mechanism: what to actually do, and how often. "Fight for the vision" landed once it came with
"someone has to hold the line" and a real behaviour attached; "once a month, look at a brand that
has nothing to do with yours" landed harder than "stay inspired". The instruction plus the how is
what makes it feel real.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy, especially listicle-style bullets and headers.
**Source:** Brand manager panel recap email, Aug 2026.

---

## Curiosity-gap negation hooks in titles ("it's not X")

**Rule:** Don't title a talk, post, or headline with an "it's not what you think" / "it's not X"
curiosity-gap hook — e.g. "Why good marketers get stuck at Manager level (and it's not what you
think)", "...it's not skills", "...it's not a brand refresh". Same negation family as the banned
"not X, it's Y" pivot, but shows up in titles rather than body copy. It's a reliable
attention-grabber precisely because it withholds the answer rather than saying anything, and
using it across more than one title in the same launch reads as a formula, not a fresh insight
each time. Name the real hook plainly instead.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy, especially talk/webinar titles and headlines.
**Source:** TMA Talk 1 title brainstorm, Jul 2026.

---

## After fixing a flagged violation, re-scan the whole draft for the same pattern

**Rule:** Catching a banned construction once doesn't mean it's gone. In the same draft, the
negation-pivot ("not X, it's Y") was flagged and fixed three separate times, reappearing each
round in a different line ("not a nice-to-have, it's the job", then "not a dashboard, not a
survey"). When a rule gets flagged, check every line in the draft against it, not just the one
called out.
**Applies to:** All Up World and Lottie copy, especially the negation-pivot ban.
**Source:** Brand manager panel recap email, Aug 2026.
