## The hook story (usable as-is, cold open material)
David's ten-year-old daughter asked him why the restaurant signage was in capitals but the menu
was lowercase — "spotted the inconsistency." He used it to explain what he does for a living via
the "draw me a pterodactyl" analogy: someone asks for a dinosaur, you draw a T-Rex, they say no,
a pterodactyl, you draw that, then they say actually mid-flight over the ocean, then actually with
someone sunbathing underneath. Point: if the brief had said all that upfront, none of that creative
time and enthusiasm gets burned.

**Usable for:** LinkedIn hook, email open, webinar teaser, course sales page anecdote.

## Teaching takeaways (structured, each stands alone)

1. **A brief isn't a manual.** Give the objective and the problem, not a paint-by-numbers spec —
   over-specifying kills the creative's ability to bring something better than what you imagined.
2. **Ask to see the agency's brief before pen goes to paper.** Their one-page distillation of your
   brief is where misalignment gets caught early — cheaply, before creative work is wasted. David
   ran this on a major beauty brand and it measurably improved hit-rate.
3. **The one-word test.** If a campaign's theme can't be reduced to one word (optimism,
   overcoming, innovation) plus a couple of reasons to believe, it's not focused enough yet.
4. **Put a number on the brief — or a range with guidance on how to read it.** No budget on the
   page means ideas nobody can afford and wasted time. A range is fine, but say what the range
   means (e.g. "top of the range only if KPIs are exceeded") or people just read the top figure.
5. **Get senior stakeholders in the room early, not after the fact.** The recurring failure mode:
   a director gives a junior a directive, the agency pushes back, the junior can't resolve it alone,
   and the director later says "I'd have agreed if I'd just been asked" — after weeks were lost.
6. **Invest disproportionate effort in the relationship, not just the brief.** David's examples:
   factory visits (seeing "stacks and stacks of products that weren't selling" made the brief real
   without needing a slide of KPIs), a Nissan motor-show stand, self-funded ski trips with a client.
   None of this is about spend — it's about time and presence.
7. **"Is this a one-way door or a two-way door?"** (Lottie's framing) — a useful question for a
   brand manager deciding whether a decision needs full stakeholder buy-in or can be made solo.
