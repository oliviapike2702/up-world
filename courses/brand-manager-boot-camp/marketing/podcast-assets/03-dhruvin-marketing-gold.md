# Podcast ep 8 — Dhruvin Patel on brand marketing without a brand manager — marketing gold

Source: `sources/transcripts/03-dhruvin-raw.txt` (recorded [DATE PLACEHOLDER — confirm record date]).
Guest: Dhruvin Patel, Founder of OkiShield. OkiShield is an eye care brand: started as a consumer
goods brand (physical products to reduce eye strain and help sleep after screen use), then pivoted
into B2B software too (a four-minute smartphone eye screening test for employees), which feeds back
into the consumer product as lead acquisition. About £5m revenue, in-house team of five (content
manager, email marketing manager, social/content lead) plus a fractional CMO and a paid ads
contractor. Serves UK and US, roughly 50/50. Recorded for the BMBC campaign. Notable: OkiShield has
no dedicated brand manager role at all, which is the spine of this whole conversation, and sits in
interesting contrast to Ruth Fittock's episode (ep. 2), which is about the risk of brand knowledge
living in only one person's head.

## The hook (usable as-is)
This episode is genuinely different from the others: Dhruvin runs a growing brand with no brand
manager at all. Lottie interviews him specifically to unpack whether that's a gap, a deliberate
choice, or brand management just happening in a distributed way across the team. That tension
("what does brand management even mean, when nobody officially owns it?") is a strong, distinct hook
compared to David's (agency relationships) and Ruth's (sole brand guardian) episodes.

## Best quotes (cleaned of filler, verbatim meaning preserved)

- "A good brand marketer basically gets the people in, then makes them remember the brand for one or
  two key things." — Dhruvin, his own compressed definition of the job.
- "Sometimes if you're on the fence, you might not be getting the right opinions." — Dhruvin, on why
  a good brand marketer needs a real point of view, even one that pushes against the brand's own
  guidelines sometimes.
- "It's a seventy-thirty rule." — Dhruvin's framing for how marketers should split their time with
  founders: 70% hitting BAU KPIs consistently, 30% proactively surfacing insight-led opportunities.
- "Different doesn't make something good... what you mean specifically is different because it's
  coming up against an age-old challenge in a fresh way." — Lottie, sharpening Dhruvin's "do things
  differently" advice into something more precise and defensible.
- "When the performance conversation is about the sprinkles, you're in a really good place." — Lottie,
  on why nailing the consistent basics matters more than chasing "extraordinary."
- "I always think, from my perspective, brand management, it depends on the company size... at the
  moment, it's everyone's responsibility to manage the brand." — Dhruvin, his direct answer on why
  OkiShield has no brand manager role.

## Teaching takeaways (structured, each stands alone)

1. **Brand management can be a distributed responsibility, not a role.** At OkiShield's size (five
   in-house marketers), everyone owns the brand's consistency rather than one gatekeeper. Dhruvin's
   view: there's "a time and place" for a dedicated brand manager once a business has enough moving
   parts, but it isn't a given at every stage.
2. **A good brand marketer protects the brand's DNA and is willing to challenge it.** Not "anything
   goes," but understanding the ethos deeply enough to know when doing something unconventional
   actually serves it, versus just copying what's already been done.
3. **A genuinely creative offline example**: to reach corporate HR directors, Dhruvin's team sent
   handwritten notes with small gifts, then followed up by phone ("did you get my care package?"),
   and separately, physically went to coffee shops and lunch spots near the target's office to find
   warm introductions. A concrete alternative to "more digital ads, more account-based marketing."
4. **The 70/30 rule for working with founders.** 70% of a marketer's time should go to reliably
   hitting BAU KPIs, no drama, compounding small improvements. The other 30% is proactively surfacing
   a data-backed hypothesis or opportunity founders don't have time to dig into themselves.
5. **A real example of spotting an opportunity from junior-level access**: a new hire taking over
   influencer outreach noticed (from being active in the DMs and on social) an entirely new audience
   segment engaging that wasn't part of the official ICP — junior team members are often closest to
   this kind of signal, precisely because they're closer to the actual customer interaction.
6. **How to brief a founder for a fast decision**: one or two pages covering the why, the how, the
   when, the exact decision needed, and how long it'll take to action, so the founder can respond
   with a plain yes or no rather than needing a meeting. Lottie's addition: ask your own founder or
   boss how *they* specifically want to receive information, since it isn't universal (she prefers
   documents over verbal, and now converts her own voice notes into a one-pager using Claude).
7. **Experimentation needs structure, not scattergun energy.** Lottie's distinction: junior brand
   energy wants to try everything in every direction; more senior brand thinking asks "what's the
   objective, what's the opportunity cost" before testing. The right answer sits between the two,
   not at either extreme.
8. **Nail the basics before chasing "extraordinary."** Lottie's closing thesis: it's rare to find
   people who consistently deliver the core requirements of their role well. Founders trust that far
   more than flashy work sitting on top of inconsistent fundamentals.
