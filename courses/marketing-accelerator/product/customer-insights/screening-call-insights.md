# Marketing Accelerator — Screening Call Insights

Sep 28, 2026 · @Liv

## Context: why the course exists

Lottie trained at P&G, went through their marketing school, and worked on huge brands with huge budgets. Her first head-of-marketing job, at Proper Corn, taught her the real lesson fast. She had the training and the track record. She had no team, no budget, and a founder who didn't speak marketing. Knowing marketing and leading it are two different skills, and nobody teaches the second one.

That's the whole premise of the Accelerator. The people who apply already know how to do marketing. What they don't have is anyone who ever taught them how to lead it, and every section below is that same idea from a different angle.

## Who applies

The stated target: a mid-level marketing manager or lead at a startup or scale-up, moving toward a leadership role, working with a small budget and a lean team. Not a corporate marketer with a big team and budget behind them. Not a junior marketer. Not a channel specialist who wants to stay a specialist.

In practice, that net is cast a little wider. B2B marketers and people at bigger, more established companies get accepted regularly, provided the underlying challenge is the same: solo or near-solo, reporting to a non-marketer, short on structure and support. Lottie runs extra check-in calls with these applicants to translate the mostly D2C course examples into their world.

## Pain points

Every point here is the same wall, seen from a different angle: excellent at marketing, never taught how to lead it.

- **Reporting to someone who's never done marketing, and doesn't really rate it.** A founder, an MD, a CEO who sees marketing as a cost, not a function. Every recommendation has to be re-argued from first principles. Every budget ask needs a KPI that doesn't exist yet, because nothing's been given the chance to run and prove itself. There's no one above them translating the work into language the business already trusts. *"I know I'm right. I just can't make them see it yet."*
- **The title changes overnight, or someone else gets it instead.** Either way, the expectation to lead arrives with no handover and no map. Some are handed the responsibility before they feel ready for it. Others watch someone less experienced get the seat, the title, or the credit that should have been theirs. *"I did the work. Why does it feel like someone else gets the win?"*
- **No peer to sense-check a decision with.** Often the only marketer in the building, or the most senior one by default. Every call gets made alone, with no one who's already sat exactly where they're sitting now. *"I don't even know who I'd ask."*
- **Every hour goes to doing, none to leading.** Running the whole function solo means execution eats the week, the campaign, the ad account, the event, the deck. Stepping back to actually lead the function feels like a luxury there's no time to take. *"I'm too busy doing the job to ever get good at leading it."*
- **Confidence knocked, skill untouched.** Something outside their control, a restructure, redundancy, time away, took the ground out from under them. The ability never left. What went missing is the belief they're still allowed to claim the room. *"My skill set is still there. Why doesn't it feel like it?"*

**Deeper layers**

The five above are the ones that recur most. These sit underneath them, less universal, still real, and worth knowing before a call goes somewhere unexpected.

- **No framework to hang a decision on.** There's no strategy, no KPIs, nothing agreed anywhere to measure against, because nobody's ever set one. Every decision gets made in a vacuum. *"I'm not just leading without training. I'm building the whole framework from nothing."*
- **Not knowing what you don't know.** Nobody senior has ever been close enough to catch a blind spot, so there's no way to know if one's sitting there unseen. *"What if the thing holding me back is something I don't even know to look for?"*
- **Freedom that's actually the absence of feedback.** A manager who's handed over the whole function and stepped back completely. It reads as trust. It also means nothing gets challenged. *"Nobody's telling me I'm wrong. That doesn't mean I'm right."*
- **Needing a credential that travels with you.** When the path up inside one company is already closed, confidence alone doesn't fix it. What's needed is proof of leadership that belongs to them, not to the employer. *"If I can't get the title here, I need something that proves I've earned it anyway."*
- **Faking authority in a room of specialists.** Chairing the conversation on a channel they don't actually understand, while the specialist across the table clearly knows more about it than they do. *"I don't need to run the ads myself. I just need to stop feeling exposed when I ask about them."*
- **Title anxiety, not ability anxiety.** The job title says exec or manager. The actual job is already bigger than that. The imposter feeling comes from the title, not the work. *"What if my title gives me away before I even open my mouth?"*
- **Reputational risk of the ask itself.** The fear isn't only whether the spend gets approved. It's whether asking for it makes them look behind, in front of the person whose opinion of their seniority matters most. *"I need this to look like growth, not like I'm behind."*
- **Torn between managing the team and growing themselves.** Less a time problem than a permission problem: whether it's even allowed to prioritise their own development over being fully present for the people who already report to them. *"Do I owe this hour to them, or to myself?"*
- **Momentum cut off by life, not performance.** A move, a redundancy, a relationship, none of it about whether they were good at the job, all of it costing them the platform they'd built. *"How do I prove I'm still that person, to someone who never saw me be her?"*
- **Regret at having deprioritised themselves once already.** Often because an employer quietly turned self-investment into a test to pass first. The lesson learned the hard way: waiting for the right moment mostly just costs time. *"I already know what happens if I wait again."*
- **Isolation with a gendered edge.** Not just the only marketer in the room. The only woman in it. *"I don't just need marketing peers. I need people who get both parts of this."*
- **Isolation from being new.** New market, new industry, sometimes new country, with none of the professional scaffolding that comes from years spent somewhere. *"Everyone else already has people to ask. I'm starting from nothing."*
- **Betting your own money on yourself.** No employer backing it, no certainty it pays off, just their own conviction that the gap is real and worth closing. *"If this doesn't work, it's my money that's gone, not theirs."*

One constant across every cohort: it skews heavily female, usually 80% or more, sometimes the whole room.

## Why now: the trigger

## The underlying want

Take away the trigger, and the want underneath is the same on nearly every call: confidence to lead, not more marketing skill. As one screening call puts it: "They know their shit. They know what they're doing. But it all comes down to... a mindset shift of how do I now think like a leader, and confidence. Like, it's being able to back yourself."

- **Confidence.** Backing their own judgement in a room with founders, boards, or anyone who doesn't speak marketing.
- **Community.** A peer group at the same crossroads, so the isolation stops being the norm. Applicants raise this unprompted, wanting people "at a similar career stage to challenge her thinking", or simply a network of others going through the same thing at the same time.

The career-stage fit is always there. What actually tips someone into applying is usually one of these, and it comes up on call after call:

- **The manager or mentor who trained them has just left**, and the responsibility has landed early, before they feel ready for it. "Very suddenly last week, she left the business... now it feels like all eyes are probably a little bit more on me."
- **They're stepping into a senior role ahead of schedule**, without the formal promotion, or the training, to go with it.
- **They're leaving, or have just left, full-time employment for freelance work**, and the leadership gap feels urgent the moment nobody's paying them a salary to carry it. "Going freelance makes the course feel more urgent, not less."

## Reservations and doubts

Four objections come up again and again, and a screener needs a straight answer ready for each:

- **"Isn't this too basic for me?"** Some applicants have seen the module titles online and worry they read as fundamentals, especially when they need to justify the spend to a boss. The line to hold: this course isn't teaching marketing basics, that's what Brand Manager Bootcamp is for. This is strategy and leadership, not "how to write a marketing strategy."
- **"Will this actually work for my B2B or bigger company?"** A recurring worry from anyone outside the core D2C/startup profile. The honest answer: the examples skew D2C, the underlying challenge doesn't, and Lottie will do extra check-in calls to translate it.
- **"Can I really do this between jobs, or while freelancing?"** Applicants who are job-hunting or just gone freelance ask whether they'll have anything real to bring to the group coaching sessions. They will, a challenge doesn't have to be a live work project. It can be confidence, direction, or imposter syndrome.
- **Budget and sign-off.** By far the most common practical hurdle. It shows up as "what's my company's ROI on this," "can I split it across two financial years," or "can I get it approved before I've even started the job." Payment plans, charity discounts and a manager's sign-off guide exist specifically to clear this.

## What resonates / tips them into a yes

- **Group coaching, not the lectures.** Ask any past cohort what they got most from the course and the answer is nearly always the same: the group coaching sessions. Live, real problems, worked through with a coach and three peers who are stuck on the same thing.
- **The community that outlasts the course.** Screeners can point to real proof of this, not a promise: cohorts keep meeting up for drinks months after finishing, unprompted, and send photos back to Lottie to show it.
- **Permission to be the only one in the room who feels this way.** Applicants consistently describe isolation before the call. Hearing that 20 other people are stuck at the exact same career stage, with the exact same problem, does more to convince them than any module description.
- **The unofficial outcome nobody puts on the website.** A large share of people either quit their job or push for the promotion they'd been sitting on, within weeks of finishing. It can't be marketed, but it's real, and it's often the thing that finally tips someone from curious to committed.

## Red flags / not-quite-right patterns

Not every applicant on a screening call is a yes. What a genuine mismatch actually sounds like:

- **Wants tactics, not strategy.** If the ask is "teach me how to grow followers by X" or "help me set up paid ads," that's not this course. Say so plainly and point them elsewhere.
- **Too junior for the room.** If someone hasn't yet reached the stage of doing marketing well and is still learning fundamentals, they need Brand Manager Bootcamp first, not Accelerator.
- **Right person, wrong moment.** Sometimes the fit is real but the timing isn't, mid-job-search with no clear direction yet, about to start a new role, between contracts with too much else going on. The honest answer here is often "yes, but the next cohort," not a hard no.
